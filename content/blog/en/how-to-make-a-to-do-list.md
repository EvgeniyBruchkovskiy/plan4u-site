It's Sunday night and you open the note called "TO DO" on your phone. Twenty-seven items. "Taxes." "Gym." "Fix the thing with the insurance." "Mom's birthday?" Some have been there since spring. You add three more, feel slightly worse, and close the app. By Friday the list is longer, not shorter.

The problem isn't you, and it isn't that to-do lists don't work. Most lists are simply built to grow forever. Here's how to make a to-do list that actually gets shorter, with a routine that takes about five minutes a day.

## Why most to-do lists fail

A bad list just moves the pressure from your brain onto a screen. The usual reasons:

- **Everything lives on one list.** "Renew passport", "learn Spanish" and "buy milk" sit side by side, so nothing stands out.
- **Items are vague.** "Taxes" is a project, not a task. You can't *do* "taxes" on a Tuesday afternoon, so you skip it.
- **Nothing has a time.** A task without a day or slot competes with everything else, every hour, and loses to whatever is loudest.
- **Today's list holds more than a day.** Eighteen items, six free hours. You fail before you start.
- **There's no closure.** Unfinished items quietly roll forward and the list turns into an archive of guilt.

Fix these five and an ordinary list becomes an **effective to-do list**.

## Master list vs. today's list

The biggest change is to stop using one list as both a storage unit and a plan.

| | Master list | Today's list |
|---|---|---|
| What goes on it | Everything you might do | Only what you'll do today |
| Size | As long as it needs to be | 3 to 5 items |
| How often you look | Briefly daily, properly weekly | All day |
| Goal | Nothing gets forgotten | Things get finished |

Your master list is a warehouse. It can hold 80 items, because you're not trying to finish it. Your **daily to-do list** is a short pick from that warehouse: the three to five things that would make today a good day.

## How to write a task you'll actually do

A good task is something you could start in the next two minutes without thinking.

### Start with a verb

"Dentist" is a topic. "Call Dr. Lee's office to book a cleaning" is an action. Call, email, buy, draft, book, pay: verbs tell your brain exactly what to do.

### Write the next physical action

Ask: what is the very first thing I'd physically do? For "plan Max's birthday party" that might be "Text three parents to check Saturday the 14th".

### Make it small enough

If a task takes more than about 90 minutes, split it. "Write the quarterly report" becomes "Outline the sections", "Pull Q3 numbers", "Draft the summary".

### Add the context you'll need

"Return the blue jacket to Target, receipt in the kitchen drawer" saves ten minutes of searching.

## Bad tasks vs. better tasks

| Bad | Better |
|---|---|
| Taxes | Download the 1099 forms from the bank website |
| Car | Book an oil change at Jiffy Lube for Saturday morning |
| Gym | Sign up for Thursday's 6 p.m. spin class |
| Christmas gifts | List gift ideas for Mom, Dad and Sam (15 min) |
| Insurance thing | Call Geico about the missing claim letter |
| Organize garage | Fill two boxes for Goodwill from the left shelf |

## How to make a to-do list, step by step

### 1. Brain dump everything

Set a timer for 15 minutes and write down every open loop: work, home, errands, replies you owe. Don't sort or judge. This becomes your master list.

### 2. Sort: do, schedule, delegate or delete

Give every item one of four fates. Quick and important: **do** it soon. Important but not urgent: **schedule** it. Someone else could handle it: **delegate**. Not worth your time anymore: **delete**, without guilt. That's the logic of the [Eisenhower Matrix](/en/blog/eisenhower-matrix/), and it often cuts a list by a third.

### 3. Pick today's 3 most important tasks

Choose three tasks that would make the biggest difference today. If you finish them before lunch, pick more. Three done beats twelve started.

### 4. Estimate the time and give each task a slot

Guess how long each takes, then add 30%. Now place each task at a specific time in your calendar: "9:00–10:00 Draft the summary". This is [time blocking](/en/blog/time-blocking/), and it turns a wish list into a plan.

### 5. Do a 5-minute daily review

At the end of the day, look at what's left. For each unfinished item, decide honestly: move it to a specific day, shrink it, delegate it or delete it. Then pick tomorrow's three.

## What to do with tasks that keep moving

Every list has one: the task moved from Monday to Tuesday to Wednesday for three weeks. Moving it again won't help. Ask why it's stuck:

- **It's too big.** Break off the first 20-minute piece and do only that.
- **It's unclear.** Write the next action, even if it's "Google how to appeal a parking ticket in Chicago".
- **You don't want to do it.** Put it first thing in the morning.
- **It doesn't matter anymore.** Delete it.

A useful rule: if something moves three times, it has to change before it's allowed to move again.

## Paper vs. app: honest pros and cons

**Paper** is fast, calm and satisfying to cross off. But it can't remind you at 4 p.m., can't move a task to next Thursday without rewriting it, and isn't with you in the hardware store.

**A to-do list app** is always in your pocket, sends reminders, repeats chores and lets you share tasks. The downside: it's easy to fiddle with features instead of working, and notifications can become noise.

A sensible mix: think on paper if you enjoy it, but keep the real list, with dates and times, in one app you trust.

## Common to-do list mistakes

- **Using the list as a diary.** Add only things you intend to act on.
- **Mixing projects and tasks.** "Kitchen renovation" is a project; "Call two contractors for quotes" is a task.
- **Never deleting anything.** A healthy list shrinks through deletion too.

## FAQ

### How many items should be on a daily to-do list?

Three to five meaningful tasks, plus small routine items. If you always finish everything, add one. If you rarely do, cut one.

### Should I write my to-do list in the morning or the night before?

The night before works for most people: you wake up with a plan instead of a decision. A two-minute check in the morning is enough to adjust.

### What's the best to-do list app?

The one you'll open every day. Look for fast entry, dates and times on tasks, reminders, and a place for undated ideas.

## Conclusion

A to-do list that works is short, specific and tied to time. Keep everything on a master list, write clear next actions, pick three for today, give them a slot and close each day with a five-minute review. Start tonight: open that 27-item note and choose tomorrow's three.

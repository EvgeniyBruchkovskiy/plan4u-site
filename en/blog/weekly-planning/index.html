How to Plan Your Week: A Simple Weekly Routine

Monday, 8:47 a.m. You sit down with coffee and open your inbox before you've decided what the week is for. By lunch you've answered forty emails, joined two calls you forgot were booked, and the dentist appointment you meant to move is still on Thursday, right in the middle of a deadline. Nothing went wrong exactly. The week just started without you.

A short weekly planning routine changes that. Thirty minutes, split into a quick look back and a plan for the next seven days, is enough to walk into Monday knowing what matters. Below: a simple method, an example week and a weekly review checklist.

Why plan your week, not just your day

Daily to-do lists are useful, but too short-sighted to carry real goals: one surprise meeting or a sick kid eats the whole day. A month is too far away to feel real; "finish the report this month" quietly becomes "next week".

A week is the sweet spot. It's big enough to make visible progress on something that matters, like a proposal, a course module or a home project. And it's small enough to see in one glance: you know which days have meetings, which evenings are busy, and when the kids have soccer.

It also gives your days context. When Wednesday falls apart, you still know what the week was supposed to deliver, and daily planning becomes a two-minute job: check the plan, adjust, go.

When to plan your week: Friday or Sunday?

There's no wrong answer. Friday afternoon works well for office workers and freelancers: work context is still fresh and you close loops before the weekend, though you may be tired and weekend plans can shift things. Sunday evening suits parents and students: you see the whole family week, but work thoughts can creep into your time off.

A popular compromise: review work on Friday at 4 p.m., then add family plans in ten minutes on Sunday. Whatever you choose, keep the same slot every week.

How to plan your week in 30 minutes, step by step

1. Review last week (5 minutes)

Open your calendar and task list for the past seven days. What got done? What slipped, and why? Carry over what still matters and consciously drop the rest. Most people skip this weekly review, yet it's what makes the next plan realistic.

2. Do a brain dump (5 minutes)

Write down everything on your mind for the coming week: work tasks, errands, calls, birthdays, bills, that email you keep avoiding. Don't organize yet.

3. Choose three weekly priorities (5 minutes)

Ask: if only three things get done this week, which three would make it a good week? Mix work and life if that's honest. For example: "send the Q4 budget draft", "book the summer camp", "run three times". If you struggle to pick, the Eisenhower Matrix is a quick way to separate the important from the merely loud.

4. Add fixed appointments (3 minutes)

Meetings, doctor visits, the parent-teacher conference, your kid's practice. These are the walls of your week. Put them in first to see how much open time is actually left.

5. Block time for your priorities (5 minutes)

Give each priority a real slot, ideally in your best hours. A two-hour block on Tuesday morning for the budget draft. Twenty minutes on Monday at lunch to book the camp. If you want to go deeper on placing blocks hour by hour, see our guide to time blocking.

6. Spread the smaller tasks across days (5 minutes)

Assign the rest of your brain dump to specific days, keeping each day light. Some people like theme days: Monday for admin, Tuesday and Wednesday for deep work, Thursday for meetings, Friday for wrap-up. Optional, but it cuts the "what now?" feeling.

7. Leave slack on purpose (2 minutes)

Keep at least one half-day mostly empty, often Friday afternoon. Things run over and the car makes a weird noise. A week planned at 100% capacity fails by Tuesday.

Your weekly review checklist

Use these questions every week:

  • What were my three priorities, and did I finish them?
  • What took more time than I expected?
  • What did I say yes to that I should have declined?
  • What's coming up in the next two weeks that needs preparation now?
  • Did I make time for sleep, exercise and people I care about?
  • What one thing would make next week smoother?

When the week goes off the rails: the Wednesday check-in

No weekly plan survives contact with reality. A client moves a deadline, your child gets a fever. That doesn't mean planning failed.

Set a ten-minute check-in for Wednesday afternoon. Ask three questions: which priorities are still on track, which one is at risk, and what can I move, shrink or drop to protect it? Often the answer is to push a "nice to have" task to next week. Moving blocks around isn't cheating; it's why you have a plan.

Common weekly planning mistakes

  • Too many priorities. Seven "top priorities" means none. Stick to three.
  • Planning tasks, not time. A list without calendar slots is a wish list.
  • No review. Planning forward without looking back repeats the same mistakes.
  • Forgetting personal life. Work fills every gap you leave open; family and health need slots too.

FAQ

How long should weekly planning take?

About 30 minutes at first, including the review. After a few weeks, 15 to 20 minutes is typical.

What's the difference between a weekly review and a weekly plan?

The review looks back: what got done, what slipped and why. The plan looks forward: priorities, blocks and tasks for the next seven days. Do them together, review first.

Should I use a paper weekly planner or an app?

Either works. Paper is great for thinking; an app is better for reminders, moving things around and sharing plans with family.

Conclusion

Planning your week doesn't need a complicated system. Look back, empty your head, pick three priorities, give them real time and leave room to breathe. Try it this Sunday or Friday, and next Monday at 8:47 you'll already know what the week is for.
